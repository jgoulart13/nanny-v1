Nobody tells you this part: the day you hire a nanny, you probably become an employer. It sounds scarier than it is. Here's what that means in Massachusetts, in plain English.

## Do you owe nanny taxes?

Almost certainly, if your nanny works for you regularly. In 2026 the line is **$3,000**: pay one household employee $3,000 or more in cash wages during the year, and Social Security and Medicare taxes apply to *all* of it. A full-time nanny crosses that in a few weeks.

A nanny who works in your home, on your schedule, doing what you ask is your employee, not a contractor. That's true even if they're also on Care.com or found you through an agency. (An agency that places a nanny is usually *not* the employer. You are.)

## What it costs: a worked example

Say you pay $25 an hour for 40 hours a week, all year: **$52,000 in wages.**

| What | Rate | You pay (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| Social Security + Medicare (your half) | 7.65% | $3,978 |
| Federal unemployment (FUTA) | 0.6% of the first $7,000 | $42 |
| Mass. unemployment (new employer) | 2.42% of the first $15,000, plus a small state assessment | about $363+ |
| Mass. paid family & medical leave | 0.46%, **withheld from your nanny's pay**, not yours | $0 |
| **Employer taxes, total** | | **about $4,400, or roughly 8.5% of wages** |
| Workers' comp insurance | Required at 16+ hours a week | Varies; get a quote |

Your nanny's share (their own 7.65% for Social Security and Medicare, plus 0.46% for paid leave) comes out of their paycheck, the same as at any job. Many families agree on a **gross** hourly rate up front so nobody's surprised.

## Federal: Social Security, Medicare and FUTA

- **Social Security and Medicare (FICA):** 7.65% from you and 7.65% withheld from your nanny, once you hit $3,000 in the year. Social Security stops at $184,500 in wages; Medicare doesn't.
- **Federal unemployment (FUTA):** applies once you pay $1,000 or more in any calendar quarter. It's 6% of the first $7,000, but a credit for paying Massachusetts unemployment brings it down to about 0.6%, or **$42 a year**.
- **Federal income tax:** you don't *have* to withhold it. You can if your nanny asks and you agree. If you don't, your nanny may need to pay estimated taxes.

You report all of this once a year on **Schedule H** with your own tax return. To avoid a big bill in April, most families raise their own paycheck withholding or pay quarterly estimated taxes.

## Massachusetts: unemployment, paid leave and income tax

- **Unemployment insurance (DUA):** you owe it once you pay $1,000 or more in a quarter. New employers pay **2.42%** on the first **$15,000** of each employee's wages, plus a small recovery assessment. You file quarterly.
- **Paid Family and Medical Leave (PFML):** households count as small employers (under 25 people), so you send **0.46%** of wages to the state, all of it withheld from your nanny's pay. There's no employer share.
- **State income tax:** if you're not withholding federal income tax, you don't have to withhold Massachusetts income tax either.
- **New hire report:** tell the Mass. Department of Revenue within 14 days of hiring.

## Workers' comp, wages and time off

- **Workers' compensation:** required if your nanny works **16 or more hours a week**. It's a separate insurance policy; payroll services and insurers can quote it.
- **Minimum wage:** $15 an hour in Massachusetts.
- **Overtime:** 1.5× pay for hours over 40 in a week, **including for live-in nannies**.
- **Rest:** at 40+ hours a week, at least 24 hours off in a row each week and 48 hours in a row each month.
- **Sick time:** up to 40 hours a year. For a one-nanny household it can be unpaid, but many families pay it anyway.

## The written agreement

Massachusetts has a Domestic Workers Bill of Rights. If your nanny works **16+ hours a week**, you must give them:

- **A written agreement** covering pay (including overtime), schedule, duties, time off, workers' comp, how to raise concerns, and notice for ending the job.
- **A timesheet at least every two weeks**, signed or acknowledged by both of you.
- **The Attorney General's Notice of Rights** (every domestic worker gets this, whatever their hours).

The Attorney General publishes a free model agreement. Honestly, it's worth doing even if it weren't required: it heads off most awkward conversations.

## How to pay: a payroll service or doing it yourself

**A nanny payroll service** (HomePay, GTM, Poppins and others) registers you with the IRS and the state, runs paychecks, files every form and sends the W-2. They charge a monthly or annual fee, and it's what most families choose.

**Doing it yourself** is possible. You'll need a federal EIN, Massachusetts DOR and DUA accounts, quarterly state filings, Schedule H, and a W-2 for your nanny in January.

## Money you can get back

- **Dependent care FSA:** if your employer offers one, you can set aside up to **$7,500** pre-tax in 2026 (up from $5,000).
- **Child and dependent care credit:** a federal credit on up to $3,000 of care costs for one child, or $6,000 for two or more. It got more generous in 2026. Money you run through an FSA can't also count toward the credit, so check which works better for your family.

Paying on the books also gives your nanny Social Security credit, unemployment protection and a record of income for renting or getting a loan. It's the right thing to do, and it's the law.

## Key dates

| When | What |
|---|---|
| Within 14 days of hiring | New hire report to Mass. DOR |
| Every quarter | Mass. unemployment + PFML filings (and federal estimated taxes, if you use them) |
| Every 2 weeks | Signed timesheet (16+ hours/week) |
| January | W-2 to your nanny and the Social Security Administration |
| April | Schedule H with your tax return |
